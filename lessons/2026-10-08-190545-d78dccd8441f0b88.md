# The Stars That Freeze While Still Blazing Hot

> **5 min · Space & astronomy · Intermediate**

## The Hook

In 2019, astronomers studying a map of nearby stars found something like a traffic jam. Certain fading stars were gathering at particular brightnesses and colours, as though they had all slowed down at the same bend in the road.

These were white dwarfs: the compact remains of stars whose central nuclear fires had gone out. They should have been steadily cooling. Instead, many seemed to be lingering.

The explanation was wonderfully strange. Deep inside them, matter was freezing—even though its temperature could still be around ten million degrees Celsius. The European Space Agency’s Gaia observations had revealed evidence of stars becoming solid. [ESA’s discovery report](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia/Gaia_reveals_how_Sun-like_stars_turn_solid_after_their_demise)

Imagine discovering that a furnace stays warm longer because something inside it is turning into ice. That is the apparent contradiction at the heart of this story.

## The Short Answer

A white dwarf can develop a crystalline core because freezing depends on the conditions of matter, including how densely it is packed. Under a white dwarf’s enormous compression, atomic nuclei can settle into an orderly solid while still extraordinarily hot.

That change releases stored energy, slowing the star’s cooling. The 2019 research connected this process to the observed pile-up of white dwarfs and inferred a cooling delay of roughly a billion years. [The original study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00370)

The star has lost its main power source, but it still has ways to stretch out its afterglow.

## How It Works

First, picture the Sun’s distant future. After its giant stages, it will shed its outer layers and leave behind its exposed core. The result will be a white dwarf, roughly Earth’s size, containing a substantial fraction of the Sun’s mass.

Its ordinary central fusion will have ended. It will shine largely by losing heat already stored inside it, rather like a stove that remains hot after someone switches it off. [NASA’s guide to stellar evolution](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/stars/)

Packing so much mass into such a small space creates conditions unlike anything in your kitchen. Ordinary atoms, with electrons arranged around individual nuclei, cease to be a useful picture of the deep interior. Instead, imagine nuclei immersed in a shared sea of electrons.

Those electrons also help keep the remnant from collapsing. Quantum mechanics limits how electrons can share the same states. Squeezing them into a smaller volume forces more of them into states with higher momentum, producing pressure that resists gravity.

A rough analogy is a theatre with strict seating rules. Once the lower rows are occupied, newcomers must take seats farther up. Compression keeps forcing the electron population into more demanding arrangements. Physicists call the resulting support **electron degeneracy pressure**. [NASA’s white dwarf explanation](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/science/objects/dwarfs2.html)

Meanwhile, the nuclei have their own struggle. Heat keeps them moving; electrical forces make them interact strongly with their neighbours. As the interior cools, motion becomes less able to disrupt an orderly arrangement.

Eventually, nuclei settle into a repeating pattern: a crystal. For the carbon-and-oxygen cores examined in the 2019 study, this is a transition from liquid to solid. “Frozen” describes the arrangement and behaviour of the material, not whether a human would find it cold.

Here the kitchen becomes useful again. When water freezes, it releases **latent heat**. Removing energy does not immediately lower its temperature further; energy must first be removed as the liquid becomes solid. A freezing pond can therefore lose heat while remaining near its freezing point.

A crystallising white dwarf has a comparable pause in its cooling. Energy released by the transition helps supply the heat escaping from its surface. It keeps shining while its interior changes state.

## The Fascinating Part

Nobody watched a stellar crystal grow through a telescope. The evidence came from counting stars.

Gaia measured distances precisely enough for astronomers to distinguish a star’s true brightness from how bright it merely looks from Earth. The researchers examined more than 15,000 nearby white dwarf candidates and found an excess at particular places on a diagram of colour and brightness.

Think of photographing runners along a racecourse. If everyone runs at the same steady speed, the runners spread out predictably. Put a steep hill halfway along, and more runners appear there because each spends longer crossing that stretch.

The stellar pile-up worked similarly. White dwarfs spend extra time at certain stages while crystallising, so a snapshot of the population catches more of them there. The observation was the crowd; the explanation came from comparing it with physical models. That distinction matters: astronomers inferred an invisible interior process from its visible effect on an entire population.

This also complicates their use as clocks. A white dwarf’s fading light carries information about how long it has been cooling. If its cooling slows, a model that overlooks the slowdown can underestimate its age. Dating a stellar population requires understanding the clock’s mechanism, including its pauses.

Then the story acquired another twist.

A 2024 study modelled an unusually delayed population of white dwarfs. For certain core compositions, newly formed crystals contain fewer heavy impurities than the surrounding liquid. They can therefore float upward, displacing heavier liquid downward.

That rearrangement releases gravitational energy. Imagine a weight descending and powering a mechanism: gravity supplies energy as mass moves into a lower position. Inside these remnants, the process can sustain brightness and interrupt cooling for billions of years. The researchers found that their model explained the unusual population’s observed properties. [The 2024 research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04419)

This floating-crystal mechanism is a model-supported explanation for particular stars, rather than a rule for every white dwarf. Ordinary crystallisation and this exceptional delay should not be treated as identical phenomena.

## Think About This

If a star can keep shining for billions of years after its central nuclear furnace shuts down, what should we really mean when we call it “dead”?

The word marks a major change in its life, but it can hide the activity that follows. Stored heat, changing material and gravity still have a long story to tell.

## One More Thing

You may encounter white dwarfs described as enormous diamonds. That comparison captures something real: carbon can be part of a crystalline stellar interior.

But a jeweller’s diamond consists of carbon atoms held in a particular structure by chemical bonds. A white dwarf’s deep crystal involves charged nuclei in extremely compressed matter, surrounded by electrons. “Diamond star” is an evocative analogy, not a literal description of a gemstone waiting to be cut.

The more remarkable picture is a star whose fading light reveals matter quietly rearranging itself far below the surface. A tiny point in the sky can contain a solidifying world—and its brightness can tell us that the transformation is happening.
