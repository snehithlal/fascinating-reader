# The Lava Lamps That Help Keep Internet Secrets

> **5 min · Computers & the internet · Intermediate**

## The Hook

Imagine visiting an internet infrastructure company and finding a wall of lava lamps. Colored blobs stretch, separate, and drift upward. A camera watches them with the patience of someone waiting for a very slow sporting event.

You might assume this is office decoration. At Cloudflare’s San Francisco office, however, the camera has a job: its images contribute randomness to a system called LavaRand, which helps protect encrypted internet connections. The lamps are an extra source of unpredictability for computers that already have other sources. [Cloudflare’s explanation](https://blog.cloudflare.com/randomness-101-lavarand-in-production/)

That raises a wonderfully awkward question. Computers can calculate trajectories, simulate weather, and search enormous databases. Why would they need help from a shelf of glowing blobs?

Because doing exactly what you are told is sometimes a security problem.

## The Short Answer

Encryption needs secret values that an attacker cannot predict. An ordinary deterministic program follows rules, so identical starting conditions produce identical results; secure random generators therefore need an unpredictable starting input. Cloudflare captures physical unpredictability with a camera and mixes it with other randomness, giving its systems an additional layer of protection. [Cloudflare’s technical account](https://blog.cloudflare.com/lavarand-in-production-the-nitty-gritty-technical-details/)

## How It Works

Start with a combination lock. A lock with many possible combinations sounds secure, but that advantage disappears if everyone chooses their birthday. The mechanism may be excellent; the choice of secret is weak.

Encryption has a similar dependency. A cryptographic key is a numerical secret used in mathematical operations that protect information. Making keys difficult to guess requires reliable randomness. Otherwise, an attacker may search the small set of values your system was likely to choose instead of tackling every theoretical possibility.

But “looks random” is a slippery standard.

Imagine a machine printing this sequence:

`0 1 0 1 0 1 0 1`

It produces equal numbers of zeros and ones. That sounds balanced. Yet you can predict its next output without breaking a sweat. Cryptographic randomness demands something stronger: even after seeing previous outputs, an attacker should not be able to predict the next one. NIST explicitly distinguishes this unpredictability from simpler statistical properties. [NIST’s randomness guidance](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-22.pdf)

Now imagine a more elaborate program that scrambles numbers through a complicated recipe. Its output might look beautifully irregular. But if an attacker knows both the recipe and its starting value, they can run the same calculation themselves.

That starting value is called a **seed**. Think of it as the initial position of an elaborate mechanical maze: once the position is fixed, the subsequent path follows the machinery’s rules.

A **cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator** uses an unpredictable seed to produce a much longer stream of values that are computationally difficult to predict. “Pseudorandom” means the stream comes from an algorithm; “cryptographically secure” means it is designed to withstand an attacker trying to discover the pattern. The seed must remain secret and unpredictable. [NIST’s randomness guidance](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-22.pdf)

This is the useful partnership: physical processes supply uncertainty, and software supplies speed. The algorithm does not magically manufacture fresh physical randomness. It stretches a carefully obtained secret into useful outputs whose prediction should require impractical computation.

The lamps enter the story at the physical end of that partnership.

A digital photograph is already numerical data. Every pixel records values representing brightness and color. Changing blobs produce changing measurements, and tiny fluctuations in the camera’s own sensor contribute uncertainty too. Cloudflare’s published design combines this camera input with randomness on an office server, then mixes that result with another source in a data center. [LavaRand’s design](https://blog.cloudflare.com/lavarand-in-production-the-nitty-gritty-technical-details/)

The photograph is raw material. It passes through a system designed to turn uncertain measurements into usable cryptographic input.

## The Fascinating Part

The security value of randomness depends on what the attacker knows.

Suppose you shuffle a deck behind a curtain. To someone outside, the next card is uncertain. To someone watching the entire shuffle with perfect tracking, it may be obvious. The deck has not changed; the observer’s information has.

That is why a colorful picture alone cannot establish security. An image can contain millions of numerical values while revealing little that an attacker cannot already predict. **Lots of data and lots of unpredictability are different things.** In cryptography, this useful uncertainty is called *entropy*.

Cloudflare’s later account describes processing camera images into compact inputs for random generators and using physical displays in several offices. The installations make an otherwise invisible computing requirement visible: machines need a supply of things outsiders cannot reliably foresee. [Cloudflare’s account of office chaos](https://blog.cloudflare.com/harnessing-office-chaos/)

Return to our imaginary visitor. They now ask the obvious practical question: what happens if someone covers the camera?

In Cloudflare’s published architecture, the other randomness sources still contribute. The design deliberately mixes sources so that losing the camera’s contribution does not automatically make the final output predictable. Its protection depends on the remaining sources and the mixing system continuing to work securely. [LavaRand’s security analysis](https://blog.cloudflare.com/lavarand-in-production-the-nitty-gritty-technical-details/)

The lamps therefore embody a broader engineering habit: combine components with different possible weaknesses. A visible physical process provides extra insurance alongside the computer’s own mechanisms.

The strange part is that reliable computing needs both opposites. We want processors to follow instructions precisely, while we want certain secrets to resist prediction. The glowing blobs help bridge those requirements.

## Think About This

If a sequence looks completely random to you but someone else can predict every value, what would convince you that it is safe to use as a secret?

## One More Thing

Lava lamps are only one way to borrow uncertainty from the physical world. RANDOM.ORG uses radio receivers tuned between stations to collect atmospheric noise and generate random numbers. A sound you would normally dismiss as annoying static becomes useful numerical raw material. [RANDOM.ORG’s technical FAQ](https://www.random.org/faq/)

Somewhere between a drifting blob and a crackling radio, the physical world supplies something an obedient algorithm cannot conjure from a known starting state: a surprise.
