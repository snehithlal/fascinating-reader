# The Frog That Spends Winter Without a Heartbeat

> **5 min · Fun Facts · Intermediate**

## The Hook

Imagine lifting a layer of fallen leaves in an Alaskan forest in winter. Underneath lies a small frog, stiff and motionless. Its eyes have turned white. It isn’t breathing. Its heart isn’t beating.

You would reasonably conclude that things have gone badly for the frog.

But this is a wood frog, and you have interrupted its winter arrangements. Ice has formed inside its body, surrounding organs and spreading between skin and muscle. Come spring, it can thaw and resume being a frog. These extraordinary changes are part of its normal survival strategy. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/kova/learn/nature/wood-frog.htm)

The puzzle is more interesting than “How does a frog survive being cold?” It is this: how can an animal survive ice inside its body when freezing usually destroys living tissue?

The answer depends on a distinction that the phrase *frozen solid* tends to hide: **ice between cells and ice inside cells are very different problems.**

## The Short Answer

Wood frogs survive freezing by protecting their cells while allowing ice to form outside them. Their bodies mobilize glucose, a sugar, which helps keep cell interiors unfrozen and limits damage from dehydration; during the frozen state, heartbeat and breathing stop. When conditions warm, the frogs thaw and become active again. [National Park Service, Denali](https://home.nps.gov/dena/learn/nature/amphibians.htm)

## How It Works

Follow our frog backward to autumn, before its winter transformation.

A wood frog is an **ectotherm**: its body temperature changes with its surroundings. It cannot maintain a warm interior the way a mammal does. As winter approaches, it settles into decaying leaves on the forest floor. That shelter moderates the cold, but it doesn’t necessarily keep the frog above freezing. [National Park Service, Denali](https://home.nps.gov/dena/learn/nature/amphibians.htm)

When freezing begins, the liver converts stored **glycogen** into glucose. Glycogen is a storage form of sugar—think of it as a pantry of linked sugar units. The glucose travels through the bloodstream into tissues, helping protect cells before circulation stops. Core organs such as the heart and liver freeze later than the extremities and thaw earlier, preserving vital functions for longer during the transition. [National Park Service, Denali](https://home.nps.gov/dena/learn/nature/amphibians.htm)

Why does sugar help?

One danger is water loss. As ice forms outside cells, water is drawn out of them. A cell can therefore suffer severe dehydration while sitting in a body full of frozen water. There is water everywhere, yet too little remains where the cell needs it.

Imagine a grape losing water and collapsing into a raisin. That is only an analogy, but it captures the shrinking problem. Concentrated glucose helps reduce that water loss and stabilize the cells. Scientists call this **cryoprotection**, meaning protection against freezing damage. Sugar is doing a structural job here, beyond its familiar role as fuel. [University of Alaska Fairbanks](https://www.uaf.edu/news/archives/news-archives-2010-2021/alaska-frogs.php)

The frog also uses other protective substances, including urea. Its survival involves several interacting defenses, rather than a single magic ingredient. [Journal of Experimental Biology](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/12/2193/12112/Wood-frog-adaptations-to-overwintering-in-Alaska)

Think of its body as a neighborhood in an ice storm. The streets can freeze while the interiors of the houses remain protected. The distinction matters more than whether the neighborhood, viewed from a distance, looks frozen.

## The Fascinating Part

For researchers, the surprise was how much tougher these frogs proved outdoors than laboratory experiments had suggested.

In a study published in 2014, scientists followed wood frogs overwintering near Fairbanks, Alaska. Their winter shelters remained below freezing for as long as **218 days**, and the lowest temperature experienced by surviving frogs was **−18.1°C**. All frogs in the monitored overwintering sample survived. Those are results from particular animals and conditions, not a guarantee for every wood frog. [Journal of Experimental Biology](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/12/2193/12112/Wood-frog-adaptations-to-overwintering-in-Alaska)

The sheltered temperatures also differed sharply from the air above. Leaves and snow trapped insulating air, buffering the frogs against much colder weather. A frog beneath snow and a weather thermometer above it inhabit different thermal worlds. The shelter and the chemistry work together. [Journal of Experimental Biology](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/12/2193/12112/Wood-frog-adaptations-to-overwintering-in-Alaska)

Wild-frozen frogs accumulated substantially more glucose in their tissues than frogs frozen in the laboratory. Researchers proposed that autumn’s repeated freezing and thawing helped build up that protection: each freeze could trigger another sugar release, while short thaws allowed relatively little to be reclaimed. That explanation was a hypothesis supported by their observations, rather than a fully settled account. [University of Alaska Fairbanks](https://www.uaf.edu/news/archives/news-archives-2010-2021/alaska-frogs.php)

For once, repeatedly freezing something might improve its winter prospects. Your leftovers have declined to participate.

Then spring changes the scene. The forest floor thaws, and the frog becomes active before many ice-covered lakes and streams open. It can breed in shallow meltwater pools that may disappear by midsummer. Surviving winter this way also helps it arrive early for spring. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/kova/learn/nature/wood-frog.htm)

The motionless object beneath the leaves was alive all along. Its winter strategy preserved the cellular machinery needed to restart ordinary activity.

## Think About This

If a living animal can spend months without breathing or a heartbeat, what does that suggest about the difference between being alive and visibly doing the things we associate with life?

## One More Thing

The family has another cold-weather trick: wood frog fertilized eggs can tolerate freezing too. Their development pauses until the water warms again, according to Minnesota’s Department of Natural Resources. Even before a wood frog has legs, cold can put its story on hold. [Minnesota DNR](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/reptiles_amphibians/frogs_toads/truefrogs/wood.html)
