# The Nuclear Reactor That Earth Built by Accident

> **5 min · Physics · Intermediate**

## The Hook

In 1972, scientists examining uranium from a mine in Gabon found something quietly alarming: a little of the wrong uranium was missing.

Natural uranium contains a small proportion of uranium-235, the variety especially useful for sustaining nuclear fission. The sample from Oklo had slightly less than expected. Further investigation revealed something more decisive: the chemical fingerprints left when uranium nuclei split. This ore had already been through a nuclear reaction. [The IAEA recounts the discovery.](https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/news/meet-oklo-the-earths-two-billion-year-old-only-known-natural-nuclear-reactor)

Imagine buying firewood and discovering that its insides had somehow already burned while the logs were still growing.

The explanation took the investigators almost two billion years into the past. Long before humans, a deposit of wet rock had assembled the ingredients of a nuclear reactor—and switched itself on.

## The Short Answer

At Oklo, concentrated uranium and groundwater allowed a nuclear chain reaction to sustain itself naturally. Ancient uranium contained a much higher proportion of uranium-235 than uranium does today, making this unusual combination possible. Evidence from trapped gases suggests that at least one reactor operated in pulses, with heating and cooling helping regulate its activity. [Research on the fuel conditions](https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/geoscience/articles/en/10.1016/j.crte.2011.09.008/) and [the original cycling study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15525157/) explain the two pieces of the puzzle.

## How It Works

Start with the uranium itself. Uranium-235 and uranium-238 are **isotopes**: atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons in their nuclei. Chemically, they are close relatives. In nuclear reactions, that small internal difference matters enormously.

Both decay over time, but uranium-235 disappears faster. Consequently, ancient uranium had a different recipe. Around the time Oklo operated, roughly 3.5% was uranium-235, compared with about 0.72% today. The geological clock was gradually thinning out the useful ingredient. [These proportions and their explanation appear in the reactor modeling research.](https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/geoscience/articles/en/10.1016/j.crte.2011.09.008/)

Now picture a neutron entering a uranium-235 nucleus. It can cause the nucleus to split into smaller fragments, releasing energy and additional neutrons. Those neutrons can split more nuclei. That is a **chain reaction**: one event supplies the triggers for subsequent events.

The central problem is keeping enough triggers in circulation. Neutrons can escape the deposit or be absorbed without producing another split. If too many disappear, the chain fizzles out. Concentrated fuel in a sufficiently large deposit improves the chances that each generation produces another. Oklo had favorable concentrations and deposit dimensions. [The IAEA describes these geological requirements.](https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/news/meet-oklo-the-earths-two-billion-year-old-only-known-natural-nuclear-reactor)

But the surprising ingredient was ordinary water.

Neutrons released by fission move fast. In this kind of reactor, slowing them makes them more effective at causing further uranium-235 fissions. Collisions with the nuclei in water drain away some of their motion. Water therefore acts as a **moderator**, the name for a material that slows neutrons.

Think of a ball losing speed through repeated collisions. The water does not supply nuclear energy; it changes how effectively the reaction can pass its energy-releasing process from one atom to another. [The American Physical Society explains water’s role.](https://physics.aps.org/story/v14/st18)

So groundwater could help the rock heat itself. Yet that heating could also remove water from the active region, weakening the very process that produced the heat.

The reactor had the beginnings of its own thermostat.

## The Fascinating Part

Here is the real detective story: scientists reconstructed a rhythm in that ancient activity using gas trapped in minerals.

A 2004 study examined xenon associated with an Oklo reactor. Xenon is an element with several isotopes, and the researchers found a mixture that pointed to intermittent operation. Their inferred schedule was approximately **30 minutes active, followed by two and a half hours dormant**. That estimate applies to the reactor they studied; it is not a timetable established for every Oklo reaction zone. [The original paper reports the cycling result.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15525157/)

How can gas remember a schedule?

The fragments produced by uranium fission undergo further radioactive changes. Some routes produce particular xenon isotopes quickly; others produce them later. If minerals trap gas mainly during cooling intervals, the captured mixture depends on how much time passed between heating and cooling. Fast-produced gas may already have escaped, while later-produced gas is available to be caught.

It is rather like reconstructing a kitchen’s routine from a cupboard that catches different cooking smells at different times. You never saw the cook, but the residue limits the possible schedules.

The proposed physical cycle is straightforward: water enters, neutron moderation strengthens the reaction, and the rock heats. Heating boils away enough water to suppress the chain reaction. The deposit then cools, water returns, and activity can resume. The broad evidence for pulsing is stronger than our certainty about the precise timing and plumbing; details of the ancient water supply remain debated. [The APS account explains both the isotope reasoning and those uncertainties.](https://physics.aps.org/story/v14/st18)

Nothing in this arrangement needed to sense temperature or make a decision. The response emerged because heat changed the availability of an ingredient needed to generate more heat.

That is **negative feedback**: an increase in activity produces a change that pushes activity back down. At Oklo, fuel, water, and rock could collectively perform a regulating function without a single moving control rod—or anyone assigned to the night shift.

## Think About This

If the mixture of isotopes trapped in a mineral can preserve evidence of a half-hour event from almost two billion years ago, what other brief episodes might apparently ordinary rocks still contain?

## One More Thing

Oklo also helps scientists investigate whether fundamental physical constants have changed over immense spans of time. The reactor’s nuclear fingerprints provide ancient evidence against which calculations can be tested. NASA identifies this as one use of the site’s surviving by-products. [NASA’s overview of the fossil reactors](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap100912.html) describes that connection.

A rock that once heated groundwater can now help test the rules governing atoms. Its operating days ended long ago; its scientific usefulness is still unfolding.
