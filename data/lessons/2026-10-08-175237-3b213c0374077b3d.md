# The Water That Freezes Because It Boils

> **5 min · Fun Facts · Intermediate**

## The Hook

Picture a small container of water beneath a glass dome. It starts at room temperature. There is no flame underneath it, no hot plate, no hidden heating element.

Someone switches on a vacuum pump.

The water begins to boil.

That is already strange enough. But as the pump keeps working, the water loses heat. Eventually, ice appears. The process you associate with making tea has helped turn water solid.

This is a real classroom demonstration: Harvard describes room-temperature water boiling inside an evacuated bell jar, then freezing because of rapid evaporation. [Harvard’s demonstration](https://sciencedemonstrations.fas.harvard.edu/presentations/boilingfreezing-water-vacuum)

The trick is that our everyday experience leaves out a crucial character. We watch the water and the stove. We rarely notice the atmosphere pressing on both.

## The Short Answer

**Water can boil without being hot, and boiling can cool it enough to freeze.**

Boiling depends on pressure as well as temperature. Lower the surrounding pressure sufficiently, and water can boil at room temperature. Turning liquid water into vapor requires energy. If that energy comes from the remaining water, its temperature falls. [Purdue’s explanation](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/demos/demosheets/14.6.html)

There is also a special combination of temperature and pressure where ice, liquid water, and water vapor can coexist in equilibrium. It is called the **triple point**. For standard reference water, it lies near 0.01°C and 611.657 pascals—about 0.6 percent of sea-level atmospheric pressure. [NIST’s account](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kelvin/kelvin-present-realization)

The dramatic vacuum demonstration and the carefully maintained triple point are related, but they describe different situations: one is water changing rapidly; the other is three phases held in balance.

## How It Works

Start with a puddle drying on a pavement. Water molecules escape from its surface even though the puddle never boils. That is evaporation.

Boiling goes further. Vapor bubbles form inside the liquid and grow. For those bubbles to survive, their internal pressure must overcome the pressure around them. Otherwise, the surrounding liquid squeezes them shut.

Think of trying to inflate a balloon while someone presses against it. Stronger outside pressure means you must push harder from within. Reduce that outside pressure, and inflation becomes easier.

Water has a property called **vapor pressure**: the pressure exerted by its vapor when vapor and liquid are in equilibrium at a particular temperature. Vapor pressure rises as temperature rises. Boiling becomes possible when it reaches the surrounding pressure.

A stove gets water to that threshold by raising its temperature. A vacuum pump gets it there by lowering the pressure outside. Purdue demonstrates water boiling at 25°C when the pressure is reduced to about 24 millimeters of mercury. No additional heating is necessary. [Purdue’s demonstration](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/demos/demosheets/14.6.html)

Now follow the energy.

Liquid water molecules attract one another. Escaping into the gas phase takes energy, rather like paying an exit fee. When no heater supplies that energy, some comes from the water itself.

The escaping vapor carries energy away. The liquid left behind cools.

You already know a gentler version of this process: evaporation helps cool sweaty skin. Under sufficiently low pressure, water can lose energy fast enough for some of the remaining liquid to freeze. The bubbling water is spending its own heat to become vapor.

The outcome depends on the apparatus. Heat entering through the container can slow the cooling. The pump must also maintain sufficiently low pressure as more vapor appears. In some demonstrations, boiling simply stops as the water cools; in others, the cooling proceeds far enough to produce ice.

## The Fascinating Part

To understand the triple point, imagine a map with temperature running left to right and pressure running bottom to top.

Instead of countries, the map has regions where solid, liquid, or gas is stable. Lines mark the boundaries where two phases can coexist. At one particular junction, three boundaries meet.

That junction is the triple point. The map is called a **phase diagram**, and it reveals why a temperature alone cannot fully tell you what state a substance will occupy. [The Open University’s explanation](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=20276&printable=1)

Imagine three neighboring countries meeting at a single border marker. Ice, liquid, and vapor each have territory nearby, but at the marker all three can share the same conditions.

This does not mean an individual water molecule is simultaneously solid, liquid, and gas. These phases describe how collections of molecules are arranged and behave. Different portions of the sample occupy different phases.

Nor does equilibrium require a spectacular bubbling frenzy. It means there is no overall tendency for one phase to consume the others under the maintained conditions. A steady-looking sample can still have molecules moving between phases.

Scientists make that balance concrete inside a sealed glass device called a **triple-point cell**. They form a sleeve of ice around a central thermometer well, leaving liquid water and vapor in the vessel. NIST describes allowing the ice to settle for at least ten days before measuring the temperature. [NIST’s triple-point cell](https://www.nist.gov/video/triple-point-cell)

The patience matters. A freshly formed ice sleeve can contain strains and defects. For an ordinary drink, those details are irrelevant. For a temperature reference, they can affect the measurement.

That is the wonderful change of scale in this story: the same substance that sloshes around in your sink becomes a precision instrument when its surroundings are carefully controlled.

## Think About This

If a pot of water can be bubbling vigorously while growing colder, what other everyday observations might tell you less than you assume unless you also know the surrounding conditions?

## One More Thing

For decades, this quiet meeting of ice, water, and vapor helped define the kelvin, the scientific unit of temperature.

In May 2019, the definition changed to use the Boltzmann constant, a fundamental constant connecting temperature and energy. Triple-point cells still provide a practical way to calibrate thermometers. [NIST’s explanation](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kelvin/kelvin-present-realization)

So the water under the glass dome has two stories to tell. In one, it bubbles and freezes, overturning your expectations. In the other, it sits almost motionless and helps scientists agree on what a temperature reading means.

Both begin with the same overlooked fact: water responds to the conditions around it, including the pressure of an atmosphere we usually forget is there.
