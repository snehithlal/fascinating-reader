# The Animal That Builds a Glass Skyscraper

> **5 min · Engineering · Intermediate**

## The Hook

Imagine finding a delicate glass vase on the ocean floor. Its walls look woven: upright threads, horizontal threads, diagonal braces. It seems like something a patient craftsperson might have made, then accidentally dropped overboard.

But the vase grew there.

It belongs to a living animal called Venus’ flower basket, *Euplectella aspergillum*. This sponge builds a skeleton from silica, the main ingredient in ordinary glass. Its structure can remain after the animal dies, leaving behind an intricate mineral cage. [NOAA’s introduction to glass sponges](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/glass-sponges/)

In 2003, researchers discovered that some of its skeletal needles could guide light like optical fibers. Later investigations revealed another surprise: its architecture offered lessons for making structures resist collapse.

An animal that spends its life filtering seawater had become an unexpected materials laboratory.

## The Short Answer

The sponge succeeds by organizing a brittle substance at several scales. Thin layers become fibers, fibers become bundles, and bundles form a braced cage.

The useful idea is that a material’s behavior depends on its arrangement as well as its ingredients. A sheet of paper flops; roll it into a tube, and it can support a surprising load. The substance stays the same, while the geometry changes the job it can do.

In experiments published in 2020, researchers made artificial lattices inspired by the sponge. Its arrangement of diagonal braces resisted buckling better than the comparison designs using the same amount of material. The result applied to the geometries and loading conditions studied; it was not a claim that a sponge provides the best design for every building. [The original lattice study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32958878/)

## How It Works

Start with the skeleton’s smallest visible building pieces: slender needles called **spicules**.

A spicule is more elaborate than a solid glass rod. Researchers examining *Euplectella* found concentric silica layers separated by thin layers of organic material. Picture the rings of an onion, stretched into a long cylinder.

These fibers gather into bundles. The bundles form larger beams, which join into the cage’s square grid. Silica-based cement binds the assembly. A 2005 study described seven levels of organization, from tiny silica particles to the complete skeleton. [The study of the skeleton’s structural hierarchy](https://www2.nau.edu/~shuster/shustercourses/BIO%20221/Reserve%20Reading/RR4/Aizenberg%20et%20al.%202005.pdf)

This nesting matters because structures can fail in different ways.

One failure is cracking. Glass can carry substantial loads, but a flaw concentrates stress at its tip. As a crack grows, that concentration can drive further growth. Layer boundaries can interrupt or redirect a crack’s path, although the benefit depends on the particular structure.

Another failure is a change of shape. Imagine four drinking straws connected by loose hinges into a square. Push sideways, and the square becomes a diamond without any straw breaking.

Add a diagonal straw, and that movement becomes much harder. The frame now has to stretch or compress a member instead of merely rotating its corners.

Buckling is another shape problem. Press down on a thin ruler standing on its end, and it eventually bows sideways. It can lose its ability to carry the load before its material crushes. For a light framework, keeping that sideways deformation under control is a central engineering challenge.

The sponge’s cage combines a square grid with diagonal bracing in a repeating pattern. The interesting question was whether the placement of those braces made unusually good use of the available material.

## The Fascinating Part

To investigate, researchers moved from admiring the animal to testing its geometry.

They used computer simulations and mechanical tests on 3D-printed lattices. That let them compare arrangements while controlling the amount of material. Otherwise, a design might appear superior simply because it contained thicker beams.

The sponge-inspired arrangement performed best among the alternatives examined. An optimization algorithm also found that its distribution of material approached the optimum within the design space considered.

Think of having a fixed box of construction pieces. You can spend them making every beam thicker, or place some where they prevent the whole frame from bending sideways. The sponge study showed how much the second choice can matter.

That does not mean evolution calculates loads or draws blueprints. Calling the sponge an engineer is an analogy. Natural selection can preserve inherited features that help organisms survive and reproduce, without foresight or a planned destination.

There is also a valuable complication. Beautiful biological structures do not automatically outperform familiar materials in every measurement.

A separate study published in 2020 cut tiny notches into the sponge’s anchor spicules and tested their resistance to fracture. Their layered structure provided toughness benefits, but those benefits were relatively modest compared with famously tough biological materials such as mother-of-pearl. [The fracture-testing study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-14128-8)

So the convincing lesson comes from specific tests: this arrangement helps with this kind of failure. “Nature made it” is the beginning of an investigation, not the result.

## Think About This

If you had to make a structure stronger without adding any material, where would you move the material you already have?

## One More Thing

The skeleton has a second, almost science-fiction property: some spicules transmit light.

The 2003 research demonstrated optical behavior resembling that of manufactured telecommunications fibers. Remarkably, the animal forms these structures under its ordinary environmental conditions, rather than in an industrial furnace. [The original optical-fiber discovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/424899a)

A useful analogy is a transparent hallway that keeps light traveling along its length. In an optical fiber, differences in optical properties between the interior and its surroundings help confine the light.

The experiment established that the sponge’s fibers can guide light. That finding alone does not establish why the animal has that capability, or whether it uses it biologically. An observed property and an evolutionary purpose are different claims.

Yet even without settling that question, the discovery changes the way the vase looks. Its threads are layered materials. Its woven walls are a load-bearing framework. Some of its needles are light guides.

What first appeared to be a fragile ornament turns out to contain several engineering problems, solved together inside one animal.
