# The Bacteria That Breathe Through a Living Wire

> **5 min · Biology & animals · Intermediate**

## The Hook

Imagine finding that your dinner is in the basement, but the air you need to use its energy is upstairs. You cannot bring either one closer. Your solution: grow an electrical cable between the rooms.

Something remarkably like this happens beneath the bottom of lakes and seas.

In the sediment, food and oxygen can be separated by a few centimetres. For us, that is hardly a distance. For bacteria, it can span thousands of cell lengths. Yet some bacteria bridge that gap with their own bodies: long chains of cells that carry electrical current.

Researchers identified these organisms as the living conductors behind unusual sediment chemistry in a landmark 2012 study. They became known as **cable bacteria**. Their existence revealed a way for life to connect resources that never meet. [Original discovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11586).

## The Short Answer

Cable bacteria split an energy-releasing chemical process between distant parts of a multicellular filament.

Cells buried in oxygen-free sediment remove electrons from sulfide, a chemical produced during the breakdown of organic matter. Those electrons travel along conductive fibres in the filament’s envelope. At the oxygen-accessible end, they are transferred to oxygen.

The chain therefore connects an electron source below with an electron destination above. Calling this “breathing through a wire” is an analogy, but the electrical transport is literal. Experiments on individual living filaments have directly demonstrated it. [Living-filament experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5984516/).

## How It Works

Start with an easily missed fact about breathing: oxygen helps us extract energy because it accepts electrons at the end of a sequence of chemical reactions.

Your cells take electrons from food and pass them through molecular machinery. Along the way, they capture usable energy. Oxygen receives the electrons at the end, helping form water. Moving air through your lungs keeps that final destination supplied.

Cable bacteria exploit a related principle, with sulfide supplying electrons. The unusual part is the geography.

Near the sediment surface, oxygen arrives from the water above. Deeper down, it has already been consumed. Sulfide is available below, but oxygen is out of reach. A cable bacterium stretches across this separation, allowing the two ends to perform different parts of the overall reaction.

Think of a battery connected to a device. Chemical changes release electrons in one place and accept them somewhere else; a conductor links the locations. The bacteria have built the connection into their bodies.

That comparison has limits. A functioning circuit also requires charge balance, including the movement of ions in the surroundings. The filament is the electron-carrying part of a larger electrochemical system, rather than a free-standing source of endless electricity.

The internal wiring is astonishingly small. Multiple conductive fibres run lengthwise through the cell envelope. In 2019, researchers connected filaments to electrodes and measured currents through specimens up to about a centimetre long, spanning more than 2,000 cells. They also demonstrated conduction through the extracted fibre network. [Direct electrical measurements](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12115-7).

A centimetre sounds unimpressive until you remember the scale. Ordinary biological electron transfers often happen across molecular distances. Cable bacteria connect chemistry across a distance you can mark with a ruler.

## The Fascinating Part

The strongest evidence came from experiments that treated the bacteria like a circuit someone could interrupt.

Researchers placed living filaments in chambers where sulfide supplied electrons at one end and oxygen accepted them at the other. They used a light-based measurement to examine electron-carrying molecules along the filaments.

Then they removed the oxygen.

The electrical pattern changed immediately. Without the electron destination, electrons accumulated in the filament’s carrier molecules. Cutting a filament with a laser also broke the pattern. These interventions showed that the distant reactions depended on a continuous living connection. [Experiments that interrupted the connection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5984516/).

That is a beautifully concrete piece of scientific reasoning: change the endpoint, then cut the proposed pathway, and watch whether the whole system responds.

The wires themselves offer another surprise. They contain nickel.

A 2021 study found a conductive core surrounded by a protein shell. Changing the nickel’s chemical state, or selectively removing it, reduced conductivity. The metal was therefore part of the conducting machinery. [Nickel and conductivity](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24312-4).

Research reported in 2026 sharpened that picture. Using several imaging and spectroscopy methods, scientists identified nickel-rich nanoribbons within the fibres. Their structural model describes repeating nickel-and-sulfur units forming long pathways inside a protective protein structure. The European Synchrotron, where some of the measurements were made, compares the arrangement to thin conductors bundled inside a flexible cord. [Structural findings](https://www.esrf.fr/cms/live/live/en/sites/www/home/news/general/content-news/general/unveiling-the-structure-of-living-electronics-produced-by-bacteria.html).

Here, the distinction between observation and interpretation matters. The mapped nickel-rich structures are experimental evidence; the detailed molecular architecture comes from combining those measurements with other techniques and modelling.

## Think About This

If a buried cell can use oxygen several thousand cell lengths away through a shared electrical connection, where does its usable environment end?

## One More Thing

The extracted fibre network can still conduct electricity after the membranes and cell contents have been removed. That helped researchers identify the actual conducting structures: the organism manufactures a material whose electrical properties persist outside the living system. [Extracted-fibre measurements](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12115-7).

This makes cable bacteria interesting to materials scientists, though a remarkable natural conductor is only a starting point for practical technology.

For now, the discovery already changes how an ordinary patch of mud looks. Its inhabitants are connected by more than drifting chemicals and competition for nearby food. Some have extended their reach by growing wires—turning their own bodies into bridges between places where different halves of a chemical opportunity are waiting.
